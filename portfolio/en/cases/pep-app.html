From Web to Mobile: Modernizing Patient Records for Oncology Physicians

Oncology doctors need patient records in emergencies, between appointments, away from the office. The web platform couldn't do that: it was hard to use and didn't work on a phone, so in critical moments doctors worked around it. I led the design of the PEP (Electronic Patient Record) mobile app from zero to launch.

Company
Oncologia D'Or / CWI Software
My role
Sole Product Designer, end-to-end
Team
PO, Engineering, Medical staff
Platform
Mobile (iOS & Android)
Tools
Figma, FigJam, Maze

The problem

Oncology physicians manage complex, high-stakes patient cases that don't stop when they leave the hospital. When an emergency arises outside the office, they need immediate access to patient history, treatment records, and clinical notes. The existing web platform was the only option, and it wasn't built for speed, mobile use, or the cognitive load of a doctor making fast decisions under pressure.

The result: doctors were working around the system in critical moments, relying on memory or manual workarounds instead of having the right information at hand.

My role & constraints

What I owned

End-to-end design, from research and stakeholder workshops through interaction design, usability testing, beta process, and final handoff. I also built the complete style guide for the app from scratch: Oncologia D'Or had an existing brand identity, but no mobile design system, so I created one specifically for this product. I presented directly to directors throughout the process.

Constraints

The web platform already existed, which meant working within established data structures and clinical workflows while designing a completely new interaction model for mobile. The challenge was translating complex medical information into a fast, legible, thumb-friendly experience, without inventing new features.

Discovery & research

I spoke directly with oncology physicians to understand how they used, and worked around, the existing platform. The picture that emerged was consistent: the web experience was slow, cluttered, and completely unusable on a phone. In an emergency, that's a clinical risk.

I also ran stakeholder workshops to align on scope and priorities before touching any interface decisions.

Key findings

01

Mobile access in emergencies

The most critical unmet need.

02

Too much information density

Doctors needed a prioritized, scannable view for quick consultation.

03

Low trust in the tool

Doctors had learned not to rely on it when it mattered most.

The design challenge

How might we give oncology physicians fast, reliable access to patient records on mobile, in the office, in emergencies, and everywhere in between?

Key decisions

01

Mobile-first, not mobile-adapted

Rather than porting the web experience to a smaller screen, I redesigned the information hierarchy from scratch for mobile. What mattered most to a doctor in a fast consultation or emergency came first. Everything else was secondary.

02

Clarity over completeness

The web platform tried to show everything at once. The app prioritized the most critical clinical information upfront, with progressive disclosure for deeper records. Less cognitive load, faster decisions.

03

Beta process for real-world validation

Before full launch, I ran a beta with physicians, gathering structured feedback through Maze and direct observation. Several interaction patterns that worked in testing needed adjustment in actual clinical contexts.

04

Co-designing with physicians from day one

Doctors helped define the feature list from the first session, and that list became the MVP scope.

The solution

A native iOS and Android app for the PEP (Electronic Patient Record). The first screen is a dashboard with the day’s appointments and the number of reported symptoms and deaths, so doctors see what needs attention before opening a single record.

Trade-offs: designing a lean MVP

The web platform had grown to include a wide range of features over time, not all of them essential, many of them rarely used. For the mobile app, I worked with physicians to define a much leaner MVP: a focused set of features covering the core of what doctors needed in fast-paced or emergency situations, rather than replicating the full web experience.

This meant making deliberate cuts. Some features that existed on web were intentionally left out of the first mobile release. They mattered, but including everything would have recreated the same overload that made the web platform hard to use in the first place. The trade-off was scope versus usability: a smaller, sharper tool that physicians could actually rely on under pressure, with room to expand based on real usage patterns post-launch.
